=== STRONG CANDIDATE EXAMPLE ===

Role: Product Manager
Background: 3 years at tech startup, led 2 product launches
Focus: Behavioral

--- TRANSCRIPT ---

[Interviewer]: Tell me about a time when you had to make a difficult product decision with incomplete information.

[Candidate]: At my previous company, we were deciding whether to build a new analytics dashboard feature. We had limited user research - only 15 customer interviews - and our engineering team was already stretched thin. 

I used the RICE framework to prioritize. Reach was potentially high (80% of users), Impact was medium (nice-to-have, not critical), Confidence was low (40% due to limited data), and Effort was 3 engineer-months.

I decided to run a quick prototype test with 5 power users over 2 weeks before committing. The prototype revealed that users actually wanted simpler exports, not a full dashboard. We pivoted, built the export feature in 2 weeks, and saw 60% adoption in the first month.

[Interviewer]: That's a great example. How did you handle pushback from stakeholders who wanted the dashboard?

[Candidate]: Our VP of Sales was initially disappointed because he'd promised the dashboard to a key client. I scheduled a 1-on-1 with him, showed him the prototype test results, and explained that the export feature would actually solve the client's underlying need faster. I also committed to revisiting the dashboard in Q3 if the export feature didn't meet expectations. He appreciated the data-driven approach and the commitment to follow up.

[Interviewer]: Got it, thanks. Let's shift to prioritization. How do you decide what to build when you have 10 good ideas but can only ship 2?

[Candidate]: I use a combination of frameworks. First, I align with company OKRs - if an idea doesn't ladder up to our quarterly objectives, it's out. Then I use RICE scoring for the remaining ideas, but I also factor in strategic bets. For example, we might score a feature as medium priority, but if it opens up a new market segment, I'll advocate for it as a strategic investment.

I also involve the team. I run a prioritization workshop where engineering, design, and sales each get to champion one idea. This builds buy-in and often surfaces insights I missed.

[Interviewer]: Can you give me a specific example of a time you had to say no to a feature request?

[Candidate]: Yes. Our CEO wanted us to build a mobile app. It was a pet project of his. I ran the numbers: our web traffic was 95% desktop, mobile app development would take 6 months, and our core users were enterprise admins who worked from desktops.

I presented this data in our quarterly planning meeting and proposed we invest in improving our web experience instead. The CEO wasn't thrilled, but he respected the data. We built a responsive web design instead, which took 6 weeks and improved mobile web usage by 40%.

[Interviewer]: How do you measure success for your products?

[Candidate]: I define success metrics before we build anything. For each feature, I identify a North Star metric and 2-3 supporting metrics. For example, for our onboarding redesign, the North Star was "% of users completing setup in first session." Supporting metrics were time-to-first-value and activation rate.

I also distinguish between vanity metrics and actionable metrics. Page views are vanity. Engagement rate and retention are actionable. I track metrics weekly and run retrospectives monthly to see if we're moving the needle.

[Interviewer]: Last question - tell me about a product that failed and what you learned.

[Candidate]: We built a collaboration feature that let users comment on reports. We thought it would drive engagement. It flopped - only 5% adoption after 3 months.

Post-mortem revealed we'd built a solution looking for a problem. Users were already collaborating in Slack and email. They didn't want another place to check.

I learned to validate the problem before building the solution. Now I run "problem interviews" before "solution interviews." I ask users about their workflows and pain points, not about features they want. This shift has dramatically improved our hit rate on new features.

[Interviewer]: We're coming to the end of our time. Thank you for sharing those examples.

--- END TRANSCRIPT ---
